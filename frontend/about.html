1 · Project title & abstract

Leaf Diagnostics: a free tomato leaf disease classifier for Lebanese farmers

Since Lebanon's economic crisis, and the wars that have followed it, agriculture has taken a real hit — tomatoes, the country's second-most-important crop, included. Lab diagnosis of leaf disease is accurate but costs money and time many farmers no longer have. This project trains a custom convolutional neural network, from scratch in PyTorch, on a combined PlantVillage and Pakistan Tomato Leaf dataset to sort a leaf photo into one of six categories — healthy or one of five diseases — and deploys it as a free web tool anyone can use from a phone. After fixing a severe early overfitting problem, the final model reaches 95.1% validation accuracy (0.13 validation loss). The result is not a replacement for a lab, but a free, repeatable first pass that can catch a problem early.

2 · Introduction & problem statement

Why this exists

Tomatoes are Lebanon's second most important crop after potatoes. Since the economic crisis, and the wars that have followed it, agriculture has taken a real hit — and tomato growers have taken their share of that damage. Lab diagnosis of plant disease is accurate, but it isn't free, and it isn't always fast enough to act on.

This project (a final submission for the LebNet Tech Fellows AI course) is not trying to replace that lab work. The goal is to give farmers something that costs nothing and can be used constantly — a phone photo of a leaf, checked in seconds, flagged early enough that a small problem doesn't become a lost field.

3 · Methodology

Data

Training used two datasets on purpose, not by accident, totaling roughly 10,900 images across six classes. The first is PlantVillage, chosen for its clarity — clean, sharp leaf images that let the model learn to look deeply into the actual texture and pattern of a leaf rather than guessing from noise. The second is the Pakistan Tomato Leaf dataset, added deliberately to bring in the opposite: photos taken the way a farmer would actually take them, with ordinary lighting and cluttered backgrounds instead of a lab setup.

Together, the two datasets push the model to learn the leaf itself, not the photography conditions around it — which matters for a tool that's meant to work on whatever a phone camera happens to capture.

Preparing the images

Every image is resized to 224 × 224, normalized, and converted to a tensor before it reaches the network, then grouped into batches of 32. Color is kept — there's no grayscaling — because color is one of the clearest signals of disease on a leaf, and throwing it away would throw away information the model needs.

Early on, the model overfit quickly, so the training set (only the training set) gets randomly augmented on the fly: a chance of a horizontal or vertical flip, a shift in brightness and contrast, and a random rotation of up to 20 degrees in either direction. That variation forces the model to learn the shape of the disease instead of memorizing the exact pixels of a training photo.

The model

Tomato_classifier is a custom convolutional network, written and trained from scratch rather than fine-tuned from an existing model. Five convolutional layers, each followed by max pooling, shrink a 224 × 224 image down to a 128-channel, 7 × 7 map — 6,272 features — before three fully connected layers narrow that down to a single score for each of the six classes.

Every conv block is followed by ReLU and a 2×2 max pool, which is what halves the spatial size at each step.

class Tomato_classifier(nn.Module):
  def __init__(self):
    super().__init__()

    # 3 x 224 x 224 in, 32 x 112 x 112 out.
    self.conv1 = nn.Conv2d(3, 32, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
    # 32 x 112 x 112 in, 64 x 56 x 56 out.
    self.conv2 = nn.Conv2d(32, 64, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
    # 64 x 56 x 56 in, 128 x 28 x 28 out.
    self.conv3 = nn.Conv2d(64, 128, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
    # 128 x 28 x 28 in, 128 x 14 x 14 out.
    self.conv4 = nn.Conv2d(128, 128, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
    # 128 x 14 x 14 in, 128 x 7 x 7 out.
    self.conv5 = nn.Conv2d(128, 128, kernel_size=3, padding=1)
    self.pool = nn.MaxPool2d(2, 2)

    # 128 x 7 x 7 = 6272 inputs, 6 outputs (one per class)
    self.fc1 = nn.Linear(6272, 512)
    self.fc2 = nn.Linear(512, 128)
    self.fc3 = nn.Linear(128, 6)

  def forward(self, x):
      x = self.pool(torch.relu(self.conv1(x)))
      x = self.pool(torch.relu(self.conv2(x)))
      x = self.pool(torch.relu(self.conv3(x)))
      x = self.pool(torch.relu(self.conv4(x)))
      x = self.pool(torch.relu(self.conv5(x)))

      x = torch.flatten(x, start_dim=1)

      x = torch.relu(self.fc1(x))
      x = torch.relu(self.fc2(x))
      x = self.fc3(x)
      return x

4 · Implementation details & results

Training setup

Every version of the model was trained for up to 50 epochs with Adam and cross-entropy loss, but the 50 was really just a ceiling to leave training running overnight. Checkpointing saved the weights with the lowest validation loss, so the final model is whichever epoch generalized best — not necessarily the last one. The learning rate, 0.0005, was settled on after testing several values against each other.

Frameworks & deployment

PyTorch and torchvision handle the network, transforms, and datasets; Pillow handles images, especially on the production side. FastAPI serves the model behind a REST endpoint, with uvicorn as the runtime. Training happened on Google Colab, for the GPU. The frontend is plain HTML, CSS, and vanilla JS, and calls a single POST /predict endpoint.

How a scan works

Three steps happen between choosing a photo and seeing a diagnosis.

1

Upload

A leaf photo is selected or dropped in from your device, and previewed before anything is sent.

2

Inference

The image is posted to the FastAPI/PyTorch backend, where the trained CNN scores it against the six classes.

3

Reading

Scores come back, get sorted by confidence, and are rendered as the readout on the diagnose page.

No photo on hand?

Not everyone testing the site has a tomato leaf nearby, so the diagnose page also offers a small gallery of sample images you can tap instead of uploading your own. Those samples were held out of both the training and validation sets on purpose — the model has never seen them during training, so a sample result is just as honest a test of the model as a photo you took yourself.

5 · Discussion & analysis

Fixing a severe case of overfitting

The first working version of the model overfit almost immediately — it was basically memorizing the training set within two epochs. Two changes fixed it: shrinking the fully connected layer by adding more convolution-and-pooling stages, and adding data augmentation to the training set. That the fix worked — validation loss dropping cleanly to 0.13 instead of diverging — supports the underlying hypothesis: the original bottleneck wasn't the problem's difficulty, it was a linear layer with far more capacity than the dataset could justify.

Before

  • 3 convolutional layers, one pool after each
  • Linear layer with ~51 million parameters
  • Memorized the training set in ~2 epochs

After

  • 5 convolutional layers, one pool after each
  • Linear layer cut to ~3 million parameters
  • Data augmentation added to training images

Limitations

Neither source dataset was collected in Lebanon, so local cultivars, growing conditions, and disease presentation may look somewhat different from what the model was trained on. The model is also single-label — it assumes one dominant condition per leaf, so it isn't built to flag a leaf showing two problems at once. And it hasn't yet been tested in the field against real Lebanese farmers taking real photos under real conditions, only against held-out data from the same two source datasets.

Future work

The clearest next steps are field validation with local farmers, expanding the dataset with Lebanese-sourced images to close that distribution gap, adding more disease and pest classes, and exploring an on-device or offline mode for areas with unreliable connectivity.

A note on scope

Leaf Diagnostics is a screening tool, not a lab. It's meant to be something a farmer can use for free, as often as they want, to catch a problem early — not a substitute for an actual laboratory diagnosis when it matters most.

6 · Reflection on learnings

What this project was like to build

The most rewarding part was tying a technical project to something real — Lebanese agriculture, after a period that's been hard on it. Watching the pipeline go from a raw dataset to a working, deployed tool that a farmer could actually open on their phone made the machine learning theory feel like it had a point.

The biggest challenge by far was the overfitting: watching a model hit near-perfect training accuracy in a couple of epochs while validation accuracy went nowhere was a good, humbling lesson in what "capacity" actually means for a linear layer, and checkpointing on validation loss rather than trusting the last epoch turned out to matter a lot. A second, quieter challenge was a bug where training metrics looked stuck at random-initialization levels no matter what changed — eventually traced to the optimizer being handed the wrong model's parameters. It was a reminder that a silently wrong setup can look a lot like a hard problem until you go line by line.
